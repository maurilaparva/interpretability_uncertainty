\begin{abstract}

\guideline{DRAFT abstract, 210 words. Rewrite the last two sentences once the results section is final.}

\noindent Language model interfaces report confidence from the output distribution. That number is the
probability of the answer the model produced, and it says nothing about how stable the mechanism behind the
answer is. A recent line of work measures that stability instead, by asking how large an internal intervention
is needed to change the answer. We ask how such a fragility signal should be shown to people. We built a
prototype over 24 short factual questions answered by Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct and computed an approximate causal
robustness margin for each answer. The same signal is rendered five ways: baseline output confidence, a single
banded margin score, a per-layer flip curve, an interactive push-it-yourself slider, and a plain-language
counterfactual sentence. An overview scatter plots confidence against margin across the question set.
Seventeen participants worked through a self-paced session with graded reading tasks, a prediction task,
per-view ratings and a comprehension check. We report how accurately people read each representation and which
views they ranked as most useful. We also report whether they could tell a precomputed slider from a live
model, and where the vocabulary of the design broke down against the numbers on screen. The study is small and
exploratory. We read it as design evidence rather than as a test of effects.

\section{Introduction}

\guideline{Outline to write next. Target 1.5 columns.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Interfaces that report uncertainty at all report output confidence. That number says nothing about
    whether the mechanism behind the
    answer is stable. Name the gap in one paragraph.
  \item Mechanistic uncertainty: the causal robustness margin of
    \citet{komanduriMeasuringMechanisticUncertainty2026}, the smallest internal intervention that changes the
    output. Incorrect and uncertain answers have smaller margins.
  \item Contributions. One prototype that renders a single mechanistic signal five ways over the same question
    set. An exploratory study with 17 participants on reading accuracy, perceived usefulness and comprehension.
    A set of design problems the study surfaced, chiefly the scale-sense problem and the confusion between a
    live model and a precomputed replay.
  \item Say plainly here that our margin approximates the published method. On our item set it is strongly
    rank-correlated with output confidence, $\rho = 0.71$. That is a limit of the stimulus, and the paper is
    about representation either way.
\end{itemize}

Our proposal asked three questions about showing mechanistic uncertainty to people. Whether visual
representations of it help a reader build an intuition for what a model can do, whether the way it is
displayed changes how well that uncertainty is understood, and whether the answers can be turned into guidance
for the people who build interpretability interfaces. This study narrows them to the display question, and we
address them in the following form.

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1] Does the choice of representation change how accurately a non-expert reads a mechanistic
    robustness signal?
  \item[RQ2] Which representation do readers find most useful for judging whether an answer is fragile?
  \item[RQ3] What design problems does showing this signal to non-experts surface?
\end{description}


\section{Related Work}

Three bodies of work meet in this project. Interactive interfaces intervene on a model's internals. Visual
analytics shows how fragile a prediction is. Uncertainty communication studies how such a signal should be
drawn or worded for a reader. As far as we can tell they have not met before. We take each in turn and then
state where our work sits.

\subsection{Interfaces that intervene on model internals}

Visual analytics for neural models moved early from passive inspection to intervention. LSTMVis let analysts
select a span of text as a hypothesis about hidden state and match it against a corpus
\cite{strobeltLSTMVisToolVisual2016}. Seq2Seq-Vis then added what has become the canonical interaction for the
genre: change the model's internal state, and watch the output change
\cite{strobeltSeq2SeqVisVisualDebugging2018}.

The same move appears in model-agnostic tools. Prospector let analysts drag a feature value on a single
instance and read the prediction off a partial-dependence curve \cite{krauseInteractingPredictionsVisual2016}.
The What-If Tool made edit-a-datapoint-and-re-infer a standard idiom \cite{wexlerWhatIfToolInteractive2019}.
HypperSteer applied interactive perturbation to sequence models for hypothetical testing
\cite{wangHypperSteerHypotheticalSteering2020}.

For transformer language models, LM-Debugger is the closest existing system to our interactive view. It
interprets feed-forward updates in vocabulary space and lets a user configure interventions that shift the
prediction in a chosen direction \cite{gevaLMDebuggerInteractiveTool2022}. The LM Transparency Tool exposes the
input-to-output information flow with attribution to individual heads and neurons
\cite{tufanovLMTransparencyTool2024}. AttentionViz offers the global-view-plus-detail structure we borrow for
our overview-to-item drill-down \cite{yehAttentionVizGlobalView2023}. All of these are built for experts, and
they ask what a component does rather than how sure the model is. Two exceptions matter to us. Transformer
Explainer explains internals to non-experts through live interaction
\cite{choTransformerExplainerLearning2024}. Steerable Chatbots hands an end user a steering coefficient as a
slider and studies how much agency people want over it \cite{boSteerableChatbotsExploring2025}.

Why interaction at all? \citet{chengExplainingDecisionMakingAlgorithms2019} ran the comparison directly. In an
algorithmic-admissions setting, interactive explanations improved both objective and self-reported
understanding, while static white-box explanations improved only objective understanding. Interactivity also
cost participants time. \citet{rossEvaluatingInterpretabilityGenerative2021a} go further and use interactive
manipulation as the measurement of interpretability rather than as the interface under test, which shaped our
task design. \citet{kirchlerExplainabilityRequiresInteractivity2021} argue from the model side that static
attributions under-determine behaviour, so counterfactual interaction is required. Our push-it-yourself view is
that tradition applied to a fragility signal. The slider does not tune a feature or a personality. It spends
the margin.

\subsection{Showing fragility as a response to perturbation strength}

A second literature draws fragility as a curve over perturbation magnitude, almost always in input space.
Bluff visualizes activation pathways over a range of attack strengths
\cite{dasBluffInteractivelyDeciphering2020}. AdvEx gives novices a perturbation adjuster and reports accuracy
and confidence before and after the attack, which makes it our nearest neighbour in both encoding and audience
\cite{youPandaNotPanda2023}. \citet{liVisualAnalyticsNeuron2023} ask which units are most vulnerable, and that
is the per-layer question our layer-by-strength inset answers. On the modelling side,
\citet{stoehrActivationScalingSteering2024} treat the scaling factor needed to flip a prediction as the
quantity of interest. It is the computational sibling of the margin, and the reason a strength sweep is a
defensible thing to plot. What none of this work does is treat the curve as an uncertainty display for a lay
reader. It remains a debugging and robustness-analysis idiom.

\subsection{Representing uncertainty for a lay reader}

Our single-score view inherits a long argument about how to encode uncertainty honestly.
\citet{schulzDeepViewVisualizingClassification2019} visualize classification boundaries together with per-point
certainty, which is the spatial version of how far an answer is from flipping.
\citet{padillaUncertaintyVisualization2021} survey the design space our manipulation samples from.
\citet{kayWhenIshMy2016} show that lay readers reason better about quantified uncertainty in discrete,
frequency-framed displays than in abstract intervals. \citet{fernandesUncertaintyDisplaysUsing2018} demonstrate
the lesson we took most seriously. The display that best supports estimating a quantity is not necessarily the
one that best supports deciding with it. That is why we measure prediction accuracy and not only comprehension.
\citet{kaleVisualReasoningStrategies2020} repeat the result with a strategy-level qualitative analysis, which is
a model for how we coded open responses.
\citet{correllValueSuppressingUncertaintyPalettes2018} address the specific risk of our margin score, a derived
number presented as if it were precise. \citet{bisantzDisplayingUncertaintyInvestigating2005} report the
preference-performance split we see in our own ratings, where the familiar bar is comprehended well but is not
judged most helpful.

\subsection{Counterfactual explanations in plain language}

Our sentence view is a counterfactual explanation with the intervention in internal rather than input space.
DECE treats counterfactuals as the user-facing unit and adds interactions for customizing them
\cite{chengDECEDecisionExplorer2020}. GAM Coach has users slide feature values under their own constraints until
the prediction flips, which is our interactive view and our sentence view fused into one interaction
\cite{wangGAMCoachInteractive2023}. \citet{schlegelInteractiveCounterfactualGeneration2024} extend interactive
counterfactual authoring beyond tabular data. On whether the format helps,
\citet{kuhlLetsGoAlien2023} provide a controlled framework and evidence that counterfactual explanations beat no
explanation on objective performance and subjective usability.
\citet{kuhlKeepYourFriends2022} show that the closest counterfactual teaches better than a merely plausible one.
That is why our sentence reports the minimum push rather than a vivid one.

\subsection{Communicating LLM uncertainty, and reliance}

Our baseline condition is the signal this literature studies. \citet{kimImNotSure2024} found, with 404
participants, that first-person expressions of uncertainty lowered agreement with the system and raised
accuracy. \citet{boRelyNotRely2024} temper what such interventions achieve. They reduce over-reliance but
largely fail to produce appropriate reliance. That sets the bar for any new display, and it warns us against
reading a preference for one of our views as a trust improvement.
\citet{villavicencioNotAllUncertainty2026} show that the granularity at which uncertainty is expressed changes
verification behaviour and self-confidence. \citet{warrenWhenShouldTrust2026} compare indicator formats
directly. All of these measure the output distribution or its verbalization. The internal mechanism is not part
of the signal.

Two further strands informed the study rather than the views. Behavioural evaluation tools frame model weakness
as something to slice and prioritize \cite{cabreraZenoInteractiveFramework2023,robertsonAnglerHelpingMachine2023},
which is how our overview quadrants are meant to be read.
\citet{cappuccioExplanationUserInterfaces2025} survey explanation user interfaces as a design space, which we
used to check that our five views do not miss a family.

\subsection{Measuring mechanistic uncertainty}

\citet{komanduriMeasuringMechanisticUncertainty2026} define the signal we display. Their causal robustness
margin is the smallest internal intervention that changes a model's output, so a small margin marks an answer
whose mechanism is easy to dislodge. The intervention is applied to a hidden state rather than to the input,
which is what separates this from adversarial work in input space. They report that incorrect and uncertain
answers have smaller margins, which is the property that makes the margin worth showing to a reader at all.

The direction of that intervention comes from a \emph{Jacobian lens}. A language model works through its
answer in stages, and at each stage it holds a long list of numbers that carries what it has worked out so
far. A Jacobian lens asks one question about that list. If we nudge it slightly, which way does the answer
move? It returns the direction that most favours a competing answer over the current one, so a nudge along it
is the most efficient way to change what the model says. The margin is the size of the smallest nudge that
actually changes the answer, and a small margin means a small nudge was enough. Appendix~\ref{app:margin}
gives the formal statement and the approximation we use.

\subsection{Where this work sits}

The premise that internal instability is worth reporting is not ours.
\citet{marjanovicInvestigatingImpactModel2024} show that explanations shift under perturbations that leave
predictive uncertainty looking confident. What is missing is the representation question. Interactive
interpretability tools intervene on internals but never report fragility to a reader. Fragility visual analytics
reports it in input space, for practitioners. Uncertainty communication studies the reader, but only ever with
the output distribution in hand. We treat a mechanistic fragility signal as an uncertainty display, render it
five ways over one question set, and put the comparison in front of non-expert participants.


\section{Methods}

In this study we compare four ways of showing a model's mechanistic uncertainty from the inside against a
baseline that shows ordinary output confidence. For this we rely on the definition of
\citet{komanduriMeasuringMechanisticUncertainty2026}, the smallest internal change that alters the model's
answer. Finding that change means knowing what the model would say under a given nudge, which is what a logit
lens provides. It reads a nudged internal state through the model's own output vocabulary, so for every layer
and every nudge we can see the word the model would produce instead.

We applied this method to 24 short factual questions covering world capitals, chemical elements, planets, and
notable figures in the arts and sciences, with eight questions at each of three difficulty levels. For each
question we obtain the model's preferred answer and its probability, and then, at every layer and every nudge
size, the word the model would say instead. In this way we obtained a precomputed grid over questions, layers
and nudge sizes, which is what the views we study draw on. In other words, every view shows the same numbers,
and only the way they are shown changes. We give the implementation details in Appendix~\ref{app:margin}.

The design of the views relies on three terms, which we introduce as follows. A model builds its answer over a
stack of \emph{layers}, and we can nudge the state it holds at any one of them. We call the size of a nudge its
\emph{push strength}, scaled so that it means the same thing at every layer. The \emph{margin} is the smallest
push strength that changes the answer, taken over all layers, so a small margin means the answer was easy to
change. We label margins Fragile, Moderate or Robust by splitting our question set into three equal groups, so
the bands are relative to this set rather than absolute.

We chose four views that span the range of forms available, from a single number, through a chart, to direct
manipulation, to plain language. Table~\ref{tab:views} gives each one and the work it draws on. In all five we
mark the answer token with a fixed colour and the alternative with a contrasting one.

\subsection{Design and procedure}

We designed the study as a self-contained web session that anyone could run on their own machine, with no
facilitator present. The interface and the questionnaire live in the same page, so participants answered each
part right after using the views rather than from memory, and the instrument recorded their responses as they
went.

We split the session into five gated parts, so that participants could not read ahead. They began
with consent and background, then explored the interface freely and reported their first impressions. Next came
guided tasks that asked them to read a view, steer the model, and predict whether its answer was right. They
then rated the five views and closed with a comprehension check and open feedback. We give the parts in full in
Appendix~\ref{app:procedure}.

Within that structure the design is within-subjects, so every participant saw all five representations, which
were also the material they were asked to rate. We counterbalanced the order of R1--R4 in the guided part,
reused that order for the ratings, and recorded it with each response. The guided tasks are graded, and we
split their items into two sets, A and B, assigned at random, so that participants working next to each other
did not converge on the same answers.

\subsection{Pilot}

Before the main study we ran a pilot round to test the interface, improve the user experience and find bugs.
We recruited about ten people, mostly classmates, and seven of them submitted complete responses. All seven
were graduate students in HCI and computer science, three at PhD level and four at master's.

Since mechanistic uncertainty is a new topic for most of our participants, what we needed to learn first was
whether people could find the controls, follow the five parts, and read what each view was showing. We did not
change the questionnaire, which is the same in the pilot and in the main study.

All six pilot participants who wrote about their first impressions reported the same problem in nearly the same
words: the interface presented everything at once and explained too little of it. One wrote that there was
\pq{a lot of information all at once, since the interface opened at `Compare All'. I wasn't sure where to start
to parse it}. Another called the interface \pq{very `in your face' with no explanations of what it does}. Four
more reported being overwhelmed by the number of graphs on the opening screen. One of them had not noticed that
the views could be opened one at a time, and another found the interface easier the longer they used it.

Based on the feedback, we realized that participants read the first impressions section as a lack of framing, a
flaw in the study rather than as part of it. That is why we made the intent visible instead of filling it in,
since explaining the two scores up front would shape the impressions we wanted to capture. We added an outline
of the study, gave free exploration its own timed part with the views hidden until the participant presses
start, and unlocked the first impressions only after three minutes.

We changed the views themselves and the guided part as well. We folded the former pop-up quick guide into two
framing steps at its start, counterbalanced exposure to R1--R4 and recorded the order, and lengthened the
view-by-view walkthrough to six to eight minutes. We also found that the views did not render consistently across
browsers, so we fixed that and checked the interface in Chrome, Safari and Firefox before the main study. The
remaining changes were minor presentation fixes, including a linear overview axis with zoom and a mini-map
after pilot participants misread the original scale, one colour per variable across the views, plainer and
larger explanations, and clearer wording in the answer key.

Because we changed the interface this much between the two rounds, we treat only the sessions run on the final
build as the main study, and every finding we report below comes from them.

For the main study we recruited participants by convenience sampling, gathering them from an undergraduate NLP
class and within the authors' own network. Most were undergraduates (15 of 17, 88\%), the other two a master's
student and one who selected ``other''. Everyone took part voluntarily, remotely and at their own pace in a
browser. We did not collect any demographic information.

We asked each participant to self-rate their familiarity with large language models (LLMs), with mechanistic
interpretability and with uncertainty scores in machine learning (see Figure~\ref{fig:familiarity}). Taken
together, the three items place our participants as regular users of LLMs who are new to model internals. Each
uses a five-point scale on which 1 is little or no exposure and 5 is active practice or research. Familiarity
falls from LLMs, through uncertainty scores, to mechanistic interpretability, where 14 of 17 (82\%) sit at 1 or
2 and nobody reaches the top of the scale. When asked whether they had used a tool that edits or steers a
model's internal activations, 4 said yes, 3 said no and 10 (59\%) were not sure, more than said no.

\section{Discussion}

\guideline{Outline.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The representation question is separable from the measurement question. Our results speak only to the
    first.
  \item Scale sense is a design problem for any derived fragility number. Bands relative to a question set make
    a tiny absolute number read as ``Robust'', and a small push read as ``large''.
  \item Interaction buys comprehension, but it also invites over-attribution of control. Relate to
    \citet{chengExplainingDecisionMakingAlgorithms2019} and \citet{boSteerableChatbotsExploring2025}.
  \item What $\rho = 0.71$ means for the pitch. On this item set the mechanistic signal is largely redundant
    with output confidence, so the claim needs an item set where the two diverge.
  \item Reliance framing. Following \citet{boRelyNotRely2024}, a preference for a view is not evidence of
    appropriate reliance.
\end{itemize}


\section{Future Work}

\guideline{Because this is a course project, substantial future work is acceptable.}

\begin{itemize}
  \item An item set selected for divergence between output confidence and margin. That is the stimulus this
    design needs, and the one we did not have.
  \item The paper's Jacobian-lens direction and a larger model, instead of the contrastive logit-lens
    approximation.
  \item Absolute rather than set-relative bands, and a vocabulary test for the words ``fragile'' and ``nudge''.
  \item A live-computation slider, to separate the interaction from the precomputed-replay artifact.
  \item A powered between-subjects comparison of two or three views on a decision task, with appropriate
    reliance as the dependent measure. It should draw on a sample beyond one class and collect a
    visualization-literacy measure as a covariate.
\end{itemize}

\section{The approximated margin}
\label{app:margin}

We used \texttt{Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct}, which has 24 decoder layers. The model answers in a few-shot,
one-word format, and we read the first token of its answer at the last prompt position. We approximate the
margin of \citet{komanduriMeasuringMechanisticUncertainty2026} rather than reproducing it.

For an item, let $a$ be the answer token, $b$ the highest-probability alternative whose decoding differs from
$a$, and $W_U$ the unembedding matrix. We take the contrastive direction

\begin{equation}
  v = W_U[b] - W_U[a], \qquad \hat{v} = \frac{v}{\lVert v \rVert},
\end{equation}

which is the Jacobian lens of \citet{komanduriMeasuringMechanisticUncertainty2026} under $J = I$. At decoder
layer $\ell$ with hidden state $h_\ell$, and at the last prompt position only, we add

\begin{equation}
  h_\ell \leftarrow h_\ell + \alpha \lVert h_\ell \rVert \hat{v},
\end{equation}

so that scaling by the residual norm leaves $\alpha$ unitless and comparable across layers. We sweep 40 values
of $\alpha$ up to $1.5$ at every layer, recording the probability of $a$, the probability of $b$ and the
current top-1 token. The per-layer margin is the smallest $\alpha$ at which the top-1 token is no longer $a$,
refined by binary search between the bracketing grid points, and the margin of an item is the minimum over
layers. We also record the layer that achieves it and the item's percentile within the set. Tokens that are
merely a different surface form of the same answer do not count as a change.

\section{Session procedure}
\label{app:procedure}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Consent and background (A).} Consent, program level and the familiarity items.
  \item \textbf{Free exploration, then first impressions (B).} The views stay hidden until the participant
    presses ``Start exploring'', and the questions unlock after three minutes. They ask what the interface
    shows, what ``Robustness'' means in the participant's own words, and what first confused them.
  \item \textbf{Guided tasks (C).} Two framing steps come first, one identical for everyone and one that walks
    R1--R4 in the participant's counterbalanced order. The graded tasks then cover reading an answer, steering
    with the slider, and comparing the two signals. A prediction task follows, in which participants judge for
    four items whether the model's answer is right, before the answer key unlocks.
  \item \textbf{Steering experience (D) and per-view ratings (E).} Agreement items on the push interaction,
    then ratings and a ranking of the five views, with two further items probing how the vocabulary reads.
  \item \textbf{Understanding check (F), trust and open feedback (G, H).} Graded comprehension items, agreement
    items on trust and usefulness, and free-text feedback.
\end{enumerate}
