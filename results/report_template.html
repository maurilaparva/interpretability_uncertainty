Mechanistic Uncertainty Interface · user study

Final study results

How participants read, steered and judged the robustness views. Scores come from the in-app questionnaire, graded against the dataset in web/public/data/items.json.

How to read the numbers

12/17 or 12 of 17
Number of participants. There are 17 in total (7 on task set A, 10 on set B), so questions asked of one set only count out of 7 or 10.
5.94 /7
Average agreement on a 1–7 scale: 1 = strongly disagree, 4 = neutral, 7 = strongly agree. Not a score out of 10, and not a rank. The bar shows how many chose each point.
3.29 /5
Average on a 1–5 familiarity scale (background questions only): 1 = least familiar, 5 = most.
Rank 1.53
Average position when participants ranked the five views from most useful (1) to least useful (5). Lower is better.
Mental demand
Also 1–7, but 1 = very low and 7 = very high, so lower is better there.

Reading a single answer was easy

Confidence, flip word, flip layer, and steering results were read correctly by 13–17 of 17. Placing the answer relative to the others (C4) was the weak spot: 9 of 17.

Predictions mostly matched the answer key

The robust, correct answer (Berlin) was judged right by all 10, and the fragile, wrong one (W → Oxygen) was judged wrong by 15 of 17. The fragile but correct Thimphu split the room.

The plain confidence view ranked first

R0 Baseline was ranked most useful by 11 of 17 and rated highest on every item. R3 Push it came second; R2 Flip curve came last and was rated the least clear.

People want robustness shown

“I would want robustness shown next to confidence” scored the highest of the trust items, and 12 of 17 would rely on robustness at least as much as confidence for a high-stakes question.

Two ideas did not land

That the Fragile/Moderate/Robust bands are relative to this question set (F3), and that a token of the same answer is not a flip (F6), were each answered correctly by only 7 of 17.

Steering read as a replay

13 of 17 said Push it felt like replaying recorded results, and the sense of control was low. Only 6 noticed the “values are precomputed” note.

Read with care

  • Small, homogeneous sample. 17 people, 15 undergraduates, mostly new to mechanistic interpretability. Treat counts as directions, not effects.
  • View order is unbalanced. The four rotations of R1–R4 were not used equally (see the participants section), so order effects are not fully cancelled.
  • R0 is always rated first and is the familiar format. Its lead in the ranking may partly reflect familiarity and position.
  • Predictions mix interface and knowledge. Several items (e.g. the symbol W) can be answered from general knowledge; C14–C15 show how much each person relied on it.
  • R0 shows partial answers. The R0 top-words list and its caption show the model’s first token (“Th” for Thimphu, “R” for Rutherford, “Ou” for Ouagadougou). This may have pushed some predictions toward “wrong” on three of the seven prediction items.
  • Free-text grading is lenient. “99” counts for 99%, “maybe layer 22” for 22, and for push strength both the “flips at” marker value and the first slider stop past it are accepted. Click any task to see every raw answer.
